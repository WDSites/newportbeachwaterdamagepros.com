Condo Insurance and Water Damage in Newport Beach, CA

A condo leak can involve your policy, the association's policy and sometimes a neighbor's. Here is how the pieces usually fit together and what to do first.

The Short Answer

Who pays when water damages a condo

Condo water damage is rarely a one-policy problem. The building's association usually carries a master policy, and you usually carry your own HO-6 style policy (a condo unit owner policy) for what the master policy leaves out. Where one ends and the other begins depends on your governing documents and your policy wording, so read both.

In general terms, the association's side often covers the building structure and shared systems, while your side often covers interior finishes, belongings and your own liability. Sudden and accidental leaks are often covered. Gradual seepage and neglect often are not. Your insurer and your association make those calls, not a restoration company, so your job in the first hours is to stop the water, record everything and notify the right parties.

Who Owns What

Walls-in, walls-out: which side usually owns which item

The table below shows the common split. It is a starting point for your questions, not a ruling, because every association writes its own rules.

ItemOften the association's sideOften the unit owner's side
Pipes and risers inside wallsShared lines that serve several unitsA line that serves only your unit, depending on the documents
Drywall, framing, subfloorOften the structure, under walls-in languageSometimes yours when the unit boundary sits at the paint
Flooring, cabinets, counters, paint, fixturesRarely, unless the documents say soUsually your side under an HO-6 style policy
Furniture, clothing, electronicsNot part of a master policyYour contents coverage, subject to its limits and deductible
Damage to a neighbor's unitSometimes routed through the associationYour liability coverage may respond. Ask your insurer

Governing documents (often called CC&Rs) and policy wording control. Some associations insure only the bare walls, others insure to a finished-unit standard. Ask your property manager for the master policy summary.

Two Layers

How the master policy and your own policy fit together

Think of two layers. The master policy is bought by the association and typically insures the building and common areas. Your HO-6 style policy is bought by you and typically insures what sits inside your unit boundary, your belongings, extra living costs if the unit is unlivable, and liability if your leak harms someone else.

The dividing line is usually called walls-in versus walls-out. Under a walls-in arrangement the association insures the structure and you insure everything from the drywall surface inward. Under a bare-wall arrangement the line sits further out. Your declarations page and the association's documents tell you which applies.

Deductibles deserve a question too. Some associations pass part of the master deductible to the owner of the unit where the leak began. Ask your insurer whether loss assessment coverage on your policy applies.

The Unit Above

When the water comes from the unit above

A ceiling stain with a neighbor upstairs raises an awkward question: whose leak is it? Photograph the stain, note the date and ask the property manager to contact the unit above. Do not enter another unit or open shared walls on your own.

Responsibility for the source often sits with whoever owns the failed item, such as a supply line, a water heater or an overflowing tub, or with the association if a shared pipe failed. Your insurer and the other owner's insurer may sort out responsibility between them. Ask yours how that works for your policy.

The ceiling cannot wait for that conversation. Wet drywall and insulation hold moisture, and a water mitigation crew can pull out saturated material, run air movers and dehumidifiers, and log the moisture readings that support whichever claim follows.

Newport Beach note: shared walls are common here

The city's Housing Element community profile counts about 44,800 housing units and puts 34.5 percent of them in multi-unit buildings. Expect to deal with a property manager as well as an insurer, and start the notice to both early.

If you rent, the association's policy and your landlord's policy generally insure the building, not your belongings. Ask your landlord who to notify, and check now whether you hold renters coverage.

First Hours

What to do in the first hours

Order matters more than speed on the paperwork. Water damage grows while you make phone calls, so the shutoff and the photos come first.

1

Stop the water

Close the fixture valve or the unit's supply valve, and tell the property manager at once if you cannot find the source.

2

Record before cleanup

Take photos and video of every wet surface and item before anything moves, then start a list of damaged belongings.

3

Notify in two directions

Tell the association or property manager and your own insurer, and ask each what they need from you.

What Happens Next

What a restoration crew does first in a condo

Water in a multi-story building travels. A crew starts with a walkthrough using a moisture meter and a thermal imaging camera to trace wet drywall, flooring and neighboring wall cavities. Standing water goes first, through extraction units. Drying follows with air movers and LGR dehumidifiers, sometimes with small openings cut into wet walls.

Crews that follow ANSI/IICRC S500 classify the water as Category 1, 2 or 3 and the materials by drying class. Ask any company whether it works to that standard. Ask for a written scope with moisture logs, because both the association and your insurer will want to see what was wet and what was done. The documents worth keeping are:

  • Photos and video of the damage before cleanup
  • A list of damaged belongings with their approximate age
  • Moisture readings and scope notes from the restoration company
  • Every receipt, including temporary lodging and drying costs

Keep damaged items until the adjuster says otherwise. Many policies also expect you to prevent further damage, so do not hold off on drying while you wait for an answer. Once the structure is dry, water damage repair replaces drywall, flooring and paint, and your documents decide which party handles which part.

Common Questions

Condo water damage insurance questions

Does the HOA pay for water damage inside my condo?

Sometimes for the structure and shared pipes, rarely for your finishes and belongings. The answer depends on the association's governing documents and its master policy. Ask the property manager for both, then ask your insurer how your own policy fits around them.

Who should I call first, my insurer or the HOA?

Stop the water, then notify both and call a restoration company to start drying. None of those calls should delay mitigation. Write down who you spoke to, the date and what they asked you to do.

What if the leak came from my neighbor's unit?

Document the damage in your own unit, tell the property manager and tell your insurer. Responsibility between you, the neighbor and the association is for the insurers and the governing documents to settle. Do not wait on that before drying your unit.

Does an HO-6 style policy cover a slow leak behind a wall?

Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered. Gradual leaks, long-term seepage and neglect often are not. Check your policy wording and ask your insurer how it treats a leak that was discovered rather than one that burst.

I rent a condo in Newport Beach. Whose insurance covers my belongings?

The building's policies generally insure the structure, not a tenant's belongings. With 43.5 percent of occupied Newport Beach units renter-occupied according to the city's Housing Element profile, many tenants are in this position.

Check whether you carry renters coverage and ask your insurer what it includes. Photograph your belongings before anything is cleaned or moved.
